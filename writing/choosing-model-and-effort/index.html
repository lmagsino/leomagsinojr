Choosing the model, and how hard it thinks

Every seat on my factory line gets two settings: which model sits in it, and how much thinking that model is allowed per task. The model sets the price. The thinking is almost free. Here’s how I choose both for every kind of work — and why the seats that judge never get cheaped out.

The idea

One setting costs money. The other is almost free.

When people ask how I keep a line of agents inside a weekly budget, they expect a story about smaller models. It’s the opposite. On my line — the same one behind AI Light Factory — which model you pick sets the cost per token, while how much reasoning you let it spend on a task barely moves the bill. In the factory docs I put it as “model is the expensive dial and effort is the cheap one” — but you don’t need the metaphor. Two choices: the model is the price, the thinking is nearly free. Most teams get them backwards.

The failure mode I see everywhere: keep the frontier model “because quality”, then quietly lower its reasoning effort to save tokens. Now you’re paying premium rates for a judge that doesn’t think. Savings come from giving the simple work to smaller models — never from making the work that needs judgement think less.

Seat by seat

What each seat gets, and why

Every seat on the line is pinned in config — model and effort both — so lowering my session default to save tokens can’t silently downgrade a seat that judges. These are the assignments I actually run:

OrchestratorMid-tier is fineMediumThe only medium seat. It follows procedure — owns the queue, dispatches, reads one-line results. It never judges
DeveloperClaude Code, frontierHighWrites the code test-first. Its output is reviewed, but a sloppy build burns the Reviewer’s rounds

Notice the pattern: the two seats at the extremes are mine and the scripts’. Full judgement gets the frontier at max effort; pure procedure gets no model at all. Every agent seat lives between those poles, placed by how much judging it does — not by how much code it touches.

The economics

Pay at creation, not at inspection

Why so generous with effort? Because the alternative is more expensive. A defect that survives to a human costs a review, a fix, a re-review and a round trip — three or four agent sessions, plus the scarcest resource on the line: my attention. Preventing it costs one extra effort step on one session.

This is also why the review side of my setup, Three-Pass Review, scales effort by risk instead of spending uniformly: a copy fix gets Copilot and a light pass; a payments migration gets three independent checks and a reviewer brief before two humans even open the diff.

The ceiling

Degrade loudly, never overspend silently

Generous effort still needs a hard ceiling, or one bad week eats the month. The deep-review engine prices every call before making it — the API’s own token counter, assuming maximum output — and when the estimate crosses the budget, it degrades in a fixed order rather than quietly billing more:

The rest of the weekly allowance is kept by habits rather than heroics: two lanes as a hard ceiling; workers that report one status line and a file path instead of their full output; waiting in a background shell rather than in turns; test output written to files; the Guardian batched in fives. And one deliberate exception — the Developer and the Reviewer are never batched, because fresh context is the Reviewer’s product. Some spend you protect.

The payoff

Where roughly half the spend goes away

Add it up, and this setup pays for its own generosity. Against the naive setup — every seat on the frontier model at high effort, nothing batched, nothing deterministic — a typical milestone on my line lands at a bit under half the usage. And the single biggest saving isn’t a model choice at all: it’s the defect round trips that never happen, because the judging seats were never turned down.

Read the ranking, not the digits. Avoided rework beats every clever model choice, which is exactly why the judging seats stay expensive. Everything below it is the same move repeated: find procedure, make it cheap — a mid-tier Orchestrator, a batched Guardian, one-line reports, and scripts where no model is needed at all.

Measured

The settings get checked against reality

Settings you never measure drift into superstition. At the end of a milestone I run alf-stats, and the two numbers I read first are sessions per ticket and cost per merged ticket — because merging is the measurement. A draft nobody merges isn’t a cheap success; it’s an expensive failure.

$ alf-stats m2-billing-exports
------------------------------------------------------------
tickets drafted            6
tickets merged             5      merging is the measurement
sessions / ticket          3.2    target ≤ 4
cost / drafted ticket      $4.85
cost / merged ticket       $5.82
reviewer rounds (avg/max)  1.3 / 2
------------------------------------------------------------
leak check: clean — no stage sending extra work downstream

When sessions per ticket climbs well above four, I don’t lower anyone’s effort — that treats the symptom. The loop’s retro mode traces which stage is leaking work downstream: vague tickets leak into extra review rounds, a weak plan leaks into parked lanes. The fix is always upstream, and it’s almost always more effort at deciding time, not less effort everywhere else.

The model sets the price. The thinking is almost free. Judgement gets the best of both.
